I build automation for a living. A big part of my job is helping teams do more with less manual work, and lately that means AI. So I've been thinking about a question that doesn't get asked enough: if AI ends up doing a lot of the work people do today, who pays for the government?

It sounds like a strange question at first. But once you look at where the government's money actually comes from, it gets uncomfortable fast.

## The government runs on paychecks

In fiscal year 2025, the federal government spent [$7.0 trillion and took in $5.2 trillion](https://www.cbpp.org/research/federal-tax/where-does-federal-tax-revenue-come-from). The difference, about $1.8 trillion, was borrowed.

Here's the part that matters. Roughly [half of all federal revenue comes from individual income taxes](https://www.cbpp.org/research/federal-tax/where-do-federal-tax-revenues-come-from), and [payroll taxes make up about another third](https://www.pgpf.org/federal-budget-guide/). Payroll taxes are what fund Social Security, Medicare and unemployment insurance. RAND put it plainly in a report this year: in 2024, [84 percent of federal revenue came from individual or payroll taxes, both of which are collected from labor income](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4980-1.html).

In other words, the federal budget is built on people having jobs. Companies matter too, but corporate income taxes are a much smaller slice than most people assume.

## How the collapse could happen

Here's the chain I keep coming back to:

1. **AI replaces a meaningful share of jobs.** Not all at once, but steadily, across a lot of industries.
2. **Income and payroll tax revenue drops.** A model or a robot doesn't get a paycheck, so it doesn't pay income tax. It doesn't pay into Social Security or Medicare either.
3. **Costs go up at the same time.** More people need unemployment benefits, retraining, healthcare help and other support, right when there's less money coming in.
4. **The government borrows more.** We're already running deficits of almost $2 trillion a year in a strong economy. Bigger deficits mean more debt, and more debt means more interest. The Peter G. Peterson Foundation notes CBO projects [net interest growing from 14 percent of the budget in 2025 to 25 percent](https://www.pgpf.org/federal-budget-guide/) over the longer term, and that's before any AI shock.
5. **Something has to give.** Higher taxes on whoever is left, cuts to programs people depend on, or printing money. Each of those hits consumers, and consumers are what keep businesses alive.
6. **Less spending means more layoffs,** which means even less tax revenue. The loop feeds itself.

That's the scenario that worries me. It isn't one dramatic crash. It's a slow squeeze where the government's main source of income shrinks while its bills grow.

## Why AI is different from past automation

People have been predicting that machines will take all the jobs for over a hundred years, and every time, new jobs showed up. So why is this time worth worrying about?

The difference is *who* gets automated. Earlier waves mostly replaced physical and routine work. AI is coming for knowledge work: analysts, writers, support staff, junior developers, paralegals. Those are often well-paid jobs, and well-paid jobs carry the tax system. In 2019, [the top fifth of earners paid 84 percent of all individual income taxes](https://www.pgpf.org/chart-archive/0185_discretionary_spending).

RAND's researchers found the same thing. Because so much income is concentrated in high-paid, white-collar roles, [replacing those jobs with AI would have an outsize effect on federal revenue](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4980-1.html). They also found that the extra profits companies make from AI [would not fully make up for the lost labor taxes unless corporate tax rates were raised significantly](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4980-1.html). And if AI becomes cheap and widely available, prices could fall and profits could shrink too, which would cut into tax receipts even further.

There's also a quirk in the tax code that makes this worse. Bill Gates pointed it out in an essay this August: hire a person and you pay payroll taxes, but buy a machine and you can usually deduct it. As he put it, ["The tax system nudges you toward replacing people with machines."](https://fortune.com/2026/08/26/bill-gates-robot-ai-tax-replacehuman-workers/)

## The case that I'm wrong

I want to be fair here, because plenty of smart people think this fear is overblown.

- **Displaced workers find new work.** The Information Technology and Innovation Foundation argues the tax base would only erode if labor's share of income fell dramatically and stayed down for years, which they think is [highly unlikely because displaced workers don't permanently leave the labor market](https://itif.org/publications/2026/05/14/ai-not-going-reduce-labors-share-of-income-or-destroy-tax-base/). In their modeling, economic growth alone recovers the lost revenue within a couple of years.
- **AI creates jobs too.** Nvidia's Jensen Huang has [rejected the robot tax and called AI a net job creator](https://www.alexgoryachev.com/alex-posts/will-ai-take-my-job-gates-huang-robot-tax), pointing to the demand for electricians and plumbers to build data centers.
- **Other taxes could pick up the slack.** At a KPMG roundtable earlier this year, participants noted that [corporate profits could grow and consumption taxes may hold up](https://kpmg.com/xx/en/what-we-do/services/tax/business-tax/responsible-tax/will-robots-pay-tax.html) as long as spending stays steady. In countries with aging populations, automation may fill jobs nobody is around to do rather than replacing people.
- **Taxing technology could backfire.** Former Treasury Secretary Larry Summers argued years ago that a robot tax amounts to [protectionism against progress](https://fortune.com/2026/08/26/bill-gates-robot-ai-tax-replacehuman-workers/), slowing down the productivity gains that make everyone richer.

I think these are real points. History is on their side. But "it worked out last time" isn't a plan, and the speed and the type of work AI is replacing make me less sure the past is a good guide.

## What we could do about it

The good news is that this is a policy problem, not a law of physics. A few ideas are on the table:

- **Tax the machines.** Gates has proposed [taxing AI tokens and robots](https://fortune.com/2026/08/26/bill-gates-robot-ai-tax-replacehuman-workers/) to slow down replacement and fund retraining and income support.
- **Fix the incentive.** Even without a new tax, ending the gap where hiring a person is taxed but buying automation is deducted would level the playing field.
- **Shift what we tax.** RAND suggests [targeted reforms that raise demand for labor and replace revenue with sources that aren't tied to wages](https://rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RBA4392-3.html), like taxing capital, profits or consumption more and paychecks less.
- **Plan before it hurts.** The worst version of this is the one where nobody prepares, and Congress only reacts after revenue has already dropped.

## Where I land

I'm not anti-AI. I use it every day and I help clients adopt it. That's exactly why I think we should take this seriously. Our whole system assumes that most people work, earn a paycheck and pay taxes on it. If AI breaks that assumption, the system needs a new foundation, and it's a lot easier to build one before the old one cracks.
